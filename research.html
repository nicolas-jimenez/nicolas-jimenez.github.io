Quality Screening through Market Access in Public Infrastructure ProcurementJob Market Paper

August 2026

Abstract

Public infrastructure is critical for economic development, but its delivery is often difficult to ensure. Governments frequently have limited capacity to screen firms' quality before awarding contracts and to monitor their performance afterward. They may instead screen quality indirectly by making eligibility for future contracts depend on the execution of past ones. These restrictions aim to allocate valuable contracts to firms that complete work more quickly, but they also reduce competition. I ask how well eligibility rules based on past execution screen firm quality, how much this screening raises procurement costs, and how alternative rules would perform.

I study Rwanda's procurement of public infrastructure, where firms progress through categories that determine the size of projects on which they may bid. Promotion depends partly on past completed contracts. I obtain electronic procurement records for the near-universe of public infrastructure tenders in Rwanda between 2017 and 2024, and combine them with government payments recorded in Value-Added Tax declarations, which allow me to measure quarterly contract completion. I find substantial heterogeneity in project execution. Only one-third of firms' project portfolios are fully paid by their scheduled completion date, while another third are delayed by more than two years. Firm characteristics explain considerably more of the variation in execution than project or buyer characteristics. Although completion speed does not enter contract award conditional on bid rank, faster firms accumulate paid experience faster and advance through the category ladder sooner.

To interpret these findings and quantify mechanisms, I estimate a dynamic auction model in which firms differ in execution speed (quality), carry unfinished contracts, and accumulate the paid experience required for promotion. Winning adds backlog, which may increase costs, but completing the work expands future project eligibility. The model separates construction costs, markups due to limited competition, and the continuation value of winning.

I find significant differences in project completion speed: high-quality firms clear 39 percent of their outstanding work per quarter, compared with 17 percent for low-quality firms. High-quality firms value awards more because completing them expands their future eligibility sooner; removing this continuation value would reduce their share of awards by 10 percent. This screening comes at the cost of thinner competition for the largest projects. Allowing firms one category below a tender's requirement to bid when they satisfy the next category's turnover threshold reduces expected total procurement costs by 1.4 percent without increasing expected delay.

Supply-Chain Spillovers from Public Procurement Contracts

Abstract

Public procurement contracts may constitute large, project-specific demand shocks to winning firms and their suppliers. Do these shocks leave lasting changes in firms and domestic supply chains, or do their effects fade when projects end? I study this question using public infrastructure projects in Rwanda, linking administrative e-procurement records to firm tax returns and firm-to-firm VAT records. A matched design comparing winners with narrowly unsuccessful bidders yields two sets of findings. First, winning sharply raises government sales during project execution, but the effects fade after the contract period. Second, procurement reorganizes sourcing around the project site. During execution, winning nearly triples the probability of forming a relationship with an available supplier in the project district — from 0.92 to 2.50 percent — and increases the firm-level number of new project-district suppliers by approximately 14 percent. This sourcing shift persists beyond the scheduled contract end: during the 3–6 months afterwards, winners remain 15 percentage points more likely than close runners-up to purchase from a project-district supplier. However, by 9–12 months afterwards, the gap narrows and becomes imprecise.

Search Costs and Firm-to-Firm Linkages: Experimental Evidence from Trade Fairs

with Jie Bai, Lauren Falcao Bergquist, Federico Huneeus, and Yuhei Miyauchi

Abstract

Connections between firms are critical for economic growth, providing new business, opportunities for learning by doing, and access to higher-quality inputs. What prevents firms from forming these connections? One possibility is information frictions. Industrial trade fairs are a long-standing solution to such frictions, coordinating a centralized meeting point for buyers and suppliers. In partnership with the largest operator of industrial fairs in Chile, we run a two-sided randomized controlled trial at the country's largest trade fairs in aquaculture and mining. On the supplier side, we subsidize randomly selected firms to host a booth at the fair; on the buyer side, we introduce randomly selected buyers to suppliers in randomized business meetings. This two-sided randomization distinguishes whether new connections crowd out incumbent suppliers or represent new aggregate business. Combining the experimental variation with detailed administrative tax data and firm surveys, we trace the direct effects on participants and the indirect effects on their competitors and trading partners. To assess the aggregate value of these new linkages, we build a structural model of firm-to-firm matching.
